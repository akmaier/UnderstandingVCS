\begin{abstract}
Explanation requires ground truth: to verify an account of a system, we must
know its inner functioning---precisely what is missing wherever explainable AI
(XAI) is most needed. Today's study objects are either simple, with a known
but trivial mechanism, or genuinely complex, with no ground-truth mechanism
against which to check explanations. We remove this dichotomy with a real
computer architecture that is fully specified and differentiable. We
re-implement the Atari 2600 Video Computer System (VCS) as two independent
emulators, \textsc{jutari} (Julia) and \textsc{jaxtari} (JAX), validated
bit-for-bit against \textsc{xitari}: 64/64 byte-identical RAM and 64/64
pixel-identical screens on tested Arcade Learning Environment (ALE)
trajectories. A full-system soft step executes the exact hard machine
and supplies surrogate gradients through CPU registers, RAM, and ROM bytes.
We prove forward equivalence by construction and
measure zero soft-handler disagreements in
\textsc{jutari} (64 games) and \textsc{jaxtari}
(9 games spanning the exercised mapper families). Exact interventions on
the hard machine independently test these gradients. A companion study
finds that vanilla saliency can fail this test, while interventions
recover more of the causal structure. GPU batching reaches ${\sim}3.1$M
steps/s for the soft CPU core, not the full-system step. Both ports
will be released under the MIT license upon acceptance.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

When we explain a system---in explainable AI (XAI) as anywhere else---we claim
that \emph{this} part is responsible for \emph{that} behaviour. Checking such a
claim requires knowing the inner functioning independently, as ground truth.
Without it we can ask whether an explanation is plausible or convincing, but not
whether it is \emph{true}. Ground truth is the prerequisite for verification, and
it is exactly what is missing where explanation matters most.

The systems available to study fall into two unsatisfying camps. Simple,
procedural models---decision trees, rule lists, sparse linear models---have fully
known inner functioning, but there the explanation is essentially the model
itself, testing an XAI method on nothing it could get wrong. The systems XAI
does target---deep neural networks, learned agents, real-world pipelines---are
genuinely complex and have no independently known mechanism, so an attribution
can be plausible and wrong with no way to tell
\citep{atrey2020exploratory,nikulin2019freelunch}.

Neuroscience faces a similar problem. \citet{jonas2017could} asked whether the
standard systems-neuroscience toolkit---connectomics, tuning curves, lesioning,
dimensionality reduction---could recover how a MOS\,6502 microprocessor computes.
The chip was chosen because its mechanism is complex and completely known.
Even with perfect data the methods produced meaningful-looking structure but
did not recover the actual organisation. This suggests testing methods against
known mechanisms. Yet the processor was not differentiable, excluding modern
gradient-based XAI. The MOS\,6502 is the CPU at the heart of the Atari 2600
VCS, the platform on which modern deep reinforcement learning was demonstrated
\citep{mnih2013playing,mnih2015human,bellemare2013arcade}.

We therefore build a study object that is \emph{complex}, \emph{fully specified},
and \emph{differentiable}. The VCS combines a 6507 CPU executing cartridge ROM,
a Television Interface Adapter (TIA) generating pixels cycle by cycle, and
a RIOT chip providing RAM, timers, and input. We implement two independent
ports: \textsc{jutari} in Julia \citep{bezanson2017julia} and
\textsc{jaxtari} in JAX \citep{bradbury2018jax}. Both have bit-exact
\emph{hard} execution and differentiable \emph{soft} CPU handlers, checked
against \textsc{xitari} \citep{xitari}, the Stella-derived C\texttt{++}
reference \citep{stella} used by the original DQN work.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Two VCS ports.} Independent Julia and JAX implementations
  reproducing reference RAM and screens on all 64 tested ALE games.
  \item \textbf{Exact-forward differentiation.} A full-system STE step
  (Theorem~\ref{thm:exact}) and bounds for relaxed execution
  (Theorem~\ref{thm:limit}).
  \item \textbf{Mechanism-grounded evaluation.} Exact interventions
  test surrogate attributions to ROM, RAM, registers, and inputs.
  \item \textbf{AI-assisted implementation.} Approximately 137 hours
  of active development time, with agent-written code checked by
  three conformance harnesses.
  \item \textbf{Reproducible measurements.} Bit- and pixel-exactness,
  gradient checks, and CPU/GPU throughput with explicit scope.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and Related Work}

The dominant XAI tools are \emph{attribution} or \emph{saliency} methods,
which assign each input element a number reflecting how much it mattered to
the output. Grad-CAM computes gradients with respect to convolutional
features \citep{selvaraju2017gradcam}; Grad-CAM++ refines their weighting
\citep{chattopadhyay2018gradcampp}. Their limitation is verification:
conventional faithfulness metrics ``need not correlate with the actual
factors responsible for the network's decision''
\citep{chattopadhyay2018gradcampp}. Validation often relies on proxies
such as bounding-box overlap or human trust.

The problem sharpens for reinforcement learning (RL) agents. DQN learns
Atari games from pixels \citep{mnih2013playing,mnih2015human}.
\citet{greydanus2018visualizing} introduced perturbation saliency
and described strategies such as Breakout ``tunneling'', but verified
one case using a deliberately inserted feature.
\citet{nikulin2019freelunch} compared built-in saliency with human
eye-tracking, a behavioural surrogate. Through counterfactual
interventions, \citet{atrey2020exploratory} showed that Atari saliency
explanations frequently fail and should be treated as exploratory.
\citet{such2019atarizoo} enabled comparisons of trained agents
without resolving mechanism-grounded validation.

Surveys repeatedly identify this gap
\citep{qing2022xrlsurvey,vouros2023xrlsurvey,cheng2025xrlsurvey,saulieres2025xrlsurvey}.
XDQN trains a transparent surrogate but measures fidelity to another
black box \citep{kontogiannis2023xdqn}. Other work supplies known
structure through inserted features \citep{yangkim2019bim}, or
small transformers compiled or trained for interpretability
\citep{lindner2023tracr,gupta2024interpbench}. Tracr and InterpBench
use known circuits as targets. Our target is instead a real
human-written program, never designed for interpretability, executing
across CPU, memory, control flow, bank switching, timers, and
cycle-timed graphics. We evaluate explanations against exact
interventions on machine variables, rather than recovery of a
designed circuit. This extends the test proposed by
\citet{jonas2017could} to gradient-based XAI.

\subsection{Known Operators and Differentiable Programming}

Our approach relates to \emph{known-operator learning}, introduced in
deep-learning computed tomography \citep{wuerfl2016deep}.
\citet{maier2019known} showed that embedding known differentiable
operators removes their error contributions from a network's bound.
The same principle appears in physics-informed neural networks
\citep{raissi2019pinn} and operator-aware deep learning
\citep{maier2019gentle}. In the VCS, every operator is known;
the task is to explain how the fixed program maps inputs to outputs.

Julia's Zygote provides source-to-source automatic differentiation
\citep{innes2019zygote}; JAX provides trace-based differentiation and
XLA compilation \citep{bradbury2018jax}. We represent ROM as a weight
tensor, RAM as an addressable tape \citep{graves2014ntm}, and discrete
decisions using deterministic softmax and straight-through estimators
\citep{bengio2013estimating}. Only the $T\to0$ relation is shared
with Gumbel-softmax \citep{jang2017gumbel}; no Gumbel noise is used.

\subsection{Why the Atari VCS Is Good Ground Truth}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\columnwidth]{figures/fig_architecture.pdf}
\caption{The Atari 2600 VCS. A 6507 CPU executes cartridge ROM over a
shared bus. The RIOT provides RAM, timer, and I/O; the TIA generates
cycle-timed graphics. Audio is not implemented in our ports.}
\label{fig:arch}
\end{figure}

The VCS (Figure~\ref{fig:arch}) is a real computer architecture, small
enough to know completely yet complex enough to be interesting.
Its 6507 CPU (a cost-reduced MOS\,6502) has a 13-bit address space;
larger cartridges \emph{bank-switch} ROM pages. The RIOT (M6532)
provides 128 bytes of RAM, an interval timer, and joystick, paddle,
and console-switch I/O. The TIA has no frame buffer: the CPU writes
registers while the electron beam scans the screen.
These include sprite colours \texttt{COLUP0}, \texttt{COLUP1},
playfield colour \texttt{COLUPF}, background \texttt{COLUBK},
and sprite-shape and position registers. The TIA produces screen
and audio signals; our ports implement the graphics path only.

ALE \citep{bellemare2013arcade} exposes the VCS to RL agents.
\textsc{xitari} \citep{xitari}, derived from Stella \citep{stella},
is the DQN reference. Given a ROM, action stream, and deterministic
initial state, its registers, RAM, and pixels provide an independent
reference for the ports.

\paragraph{Other Atari substrates and differentiable simulators.}
CuLE provides GPU Atari execution but is neither bit-exact nor
differentiable \citep{dalton2020cule}. OCAtari and JAXAtari offer
structured, GPU-oriented environments
\citep{delfosse2023ocatari,delfosse2025jaxatari}, but JAXAtari
reimplements game logic rather than executing original ROMs.
Brax differentiates continuous rigid-body physics
\citep{freeman2021brax}; we differentiate selected operations
of a discrete computer while preserving its hard execution.
We use ALE's game catalogue and evaluation protocol
\citep{bellemare2013arcade,machado2018revisiting}.

\subsection{Hard and Soft Execution}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.90\textwidth]{figures/fig_pipeline.pdf}
\caption{HARD executes each instruction on the complete machine
bit-exactly. SOFT replays the same instruction using differentiable
CPU/RAM handlers, soft state, and hard device-read constants.
A straight-through join preserves hard register and RAM values
while propagating surrogate gradients to registers, RAM, and ROM
bytes. Frame-buffer and device states carry no gradient.}
\label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure*}

In HARD mode the CPU uses integer arithmetic, bit flags, and
opcode dispatch. The full-system SOFT-STE step, implemented
in both ports, first executes the instruction on the hard
CPU, bus, TIA, RIOT, and cartridge. It then replays the
instruction through differentiable handlers against that same
hard machine. ROM reads follow the hard cartridge's current
bank/slice mapping; TIA, RIOT, and Superchip register reads
are constants from the hard bus. Writes outside RAM affect
only hard devices. Every CPU register and RAM cell is joined
with its hard value through STE. Device and frame-buffer
states remain hard and receive no gradient. Gradients reach
registers, RAM, and ROM bytes.

Table~\ref{tab:modes} distinguishes execution from gradient
methods. FULL relaxes branches and reads without STE,
rounding their results to integers; opcode dispatch remains
hard. LEAKY uses $\alpha=5.5$, $T=0.145$ just outside the
sufficient exactness region and permits mild forward divergence.
NAIVE differentiates through hard discrete indices, giving zero
position gradients. SAMPLER adds a bilinear position kernel.

The soft formulation uses three constructions. First, ROM
and RAM reads use a one-hot address vector. For memory
$r\in\mathbb{R}^{M}$ and address $a$,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{peek}(r,a) \;=\; \mathbf{1}_a^{\top} r \;=\; \sum_{i} [\![i=a]\!]\, r_i \;=\; r_a .
\label{eq:peek}
\end{equation}
The value is $r_a$, with gradient
$\partial\,\mathrm{peek}/\partial r=\mathbf{1}_a$.
This is the discrete limit of attention-style memory addressing
\citep{graves2014ntm}.

Second, a general soft selection over $K=256$ opcode
candidates has logits $\ell$ and weights
$w=\softmax(\ell/T)$:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{select}(\ell,V;T) = w^{\top} V,
\label{eq:select}
\end{equation}
where $V$ contains candidate outputs. The selection
approaches the hard winner as $T\to0$
\citep{jang2017gumbel}. This is deterministic,
without Gumbel noise. Actual opcode dispatch remains
a hard switch in both SOFT-STE and FULL.
Figure~\ref{fig:tempheat} visualises the spread of
soft selection using sprite columns, not opcodes.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig_temp_heatmap.pdf}
\caption{Temperature controls soft selection in pixel space.
A sprite column is sampled 100 times and the hard renders
averaged. Increasing $T$ spreads occupancy
($\sigma\approx1.3,3,6$ px). These averages illustrate
temperature spread, not the bit-exact STE forward pass.}
\label{fig:tempheat}
\end{figure}

Third, conditional branches use a sigmoid gate. Let
$f\in[0,1]$ be a relaxed status flag, $z=s(2f-1)$
with $s=\pm1$ for branch-when-set/clear, and
$\delta$ the relative offset:
\begin{equation}
g = \sigma(\alpha z),\qquad
\mathrm{pc}_{\mathrm{soft}} = (1-g)\,\mathrm{pc}_{\bar b} + g\,\mathrm{pc}_{b}
= \mathrm{pc}_{\bar b} + g\,\delta,
\label{eq:branch}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{pc}_{\bar b}$ is the fall-through counter.
FULL rounds the target to an integer. With an exact binary
flag, a branch rounds correctly when
$|\delta|\sigma(-\alpha)<1/2$; thus $\alpha\ge6$
suffices for 6502 offsets $|\delta|\le128$.
FULL memory reads instead use distance-softmax at
temperature $T$ and round their byte values.
Proposition~\ref{prop:finite} gives both exactness conditions.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig_sigmoid_alpha.pdf}
\caption{Sigmoid branch gate and derivative for
$\alpha\in\{2,6,20\}$, with $z=0.25$ marked.
Small $\alpha$ gives a shallow gate; large $\alpha$
saturates it. Here $\alpha=6$ gives the largest
local gradient at the marked point.}
\label{fig:sigmoid}
\end{figure}

The straight-through estimator (STE)
\citep{bengio2013estimating} joins soft and hard values:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{STE}(\text{soft},\text{hard}) = \text{soft} + \sg(\text{hard}-\text{soft}),
\label{eq:ste}
\end{equation}
where $\sg$ stops gradients. Its forward value is hard;
its derivative follows soft. Applied after every
instruction to all CPU registers and RAM cells,
it makes the complete forward state exact
(Theorem~\ref{thm:exact}), independently of the
relaxation parameters.

The key distinction is between \emph{content} and
\emph{indices}. A stored hard switch routes gradients
to selected values, as in max-pooling: register
contents, sprite colours, graphics bits, and priority
compositing. It does not differentiate which index was
selected, such as a sprite column determined by strobe
timing. A triangular (bilinear) sampler
\citep{jaderberg2015spatial} supplies a position
surrogate. Whether that gradient reflects the actual
mechanism must be tested by exact interventions.


\section{Soft Equals Hard: Equivalence and Gradients}

Let $\Phi_{\mathrm{H}}$ and $\Phi_{\mathrm{S}}$ be the
hard and full-system SOFT-STE one-step maps on the
complete machine state $x$ (CPU, RAM, TIA, RIOT,
cartridge, frame buffer). Proofs are in the supplement.

\begin{theorem}[Exact forward equivalence]
\label{thm:exact}
For every reachable state $x$ and finite $\alpha,T>0$,
$\Phi_{\mathrm{S}}(x)=\Phi_{\mathrm{H}}(x)$.
Consequently, identical initial states and inputs yield
identical complete trajectories for any number of steps.
SOFT-STE differs only in its surrogate backward rule.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The full-system step executes the complete hard machine
and replays the instruction through soft CPU/RAM handlers
against that same state. Every CPU register and RAM cell
is joined with its hard value by~\eqref{eq:ste}, while
all device and frame-buffer states remain hard.
Thus every output component equals the hard value,
and trajectory equality follows by induction.
\end{proof}

Exact forward execution does not imply exact gradients:
the STE derivative remains a surrogate of the
discrete machine.

The FULL variant instead relaxes only branches and
memory reads without STE, rounding their results
to integers; opcode dispatch remains hard.

\begin{assumption}[Decision margins]
\label{ass:dm}
Every relaxed branch on the executed trajectory has
flag-logit margin $m_t=|z_t|>0$, with
$m_{\min}=\min_t m_t>0$. Each memory read selects
a unique integer address, with other candidates
at distance at least one.
\end{assumption}

For exact processor flags, $z_t=\pm1$ and
$m_{\min}=1$.

\begin{theorem}[Temperature-limit bound]
\label{thm:limit}
For the unrounded relaxed branch and read values,
under Assumption~\ref{ass:dm}, the local deviation
from the hard operation satisfies
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\big\|\Phi_{\mathrm{S}}^{T,\alpha}(x)
-\Phi_{\mathrm{H}}(x)\big\|_\infty
\le C\big[
e^{-\alpha m_{\min}}
+\rho e^{-c/T}\big],
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:bound}
\end{equation}
where $C$ bounds value ranges, $\rho$ the number of
reads per step, and $c>0$ the read-distance gap.
The two rates remain separate:
$\mathcal{O}(e^{-\alpha m_{\min}}+e^{-c/T})$.
There is no opcode-dispatch term because dispatch
remains hard.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The sigmoid branch error is bounded by
$e^{-\alpha m_{\min}}$; incorrect-address weight
in a distance-softmax read has a geometric tail
of order $e^{-c/T}$. Bounding the corresponding
value differences gives~\eqref{eq:bound}.
\end{proof}

Rounding gives a stronger finite-parameter result.

\begin{proposition}[Finite-parameter exactness]
\label{prop:finite}
If every relaxed branch target and memory-read value
is within $1/2$ of its hard integer value, rounding
restores that value exactly. With hard opcode dispatch,
the complete trajectory is then bit-exact.

For a branch with binary flag and offset $\delta$,
the condition is
\begin{equation}
|\delta|\sigma(-\alpha)<\frac12
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
|\delta|<\frac{1+e^\alpha}{2}.
\label{eq:branchcorner}
\end{equation}
Since 6502 offsets satisfy $|\delta|\le128$,
$\alpha\ge6$ suffices (bound greater than $202$).

For a distance-softmax byte read with selected-address
weight $w_0$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
|\mathrm{read}_{T}-r_a|
&\le255(1-w_0)\\
&\le\frac{510e^{-1/T}}{1-e^{-1/T}}
<\frac12 \quad (T\le0.14).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:readcorner}
\end{equation}
The bound is approximately $0.40$ at $T=0.14$.
Thus $\alpha\ge6$, $T\le0.14$ suffice independently
of the executed program.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
A branch differs from its hard target by
$|\delta|\sigma(-\alpha)$; incorrect read-address
weights are bounded by a two-sided geometric series.
Both deviations below $1/2$ round to the hard
integers, so induction gives whole-trajectory equality.
The full proof is in the supplement.
\end{proof}

At $\alpha=6$, $T=0.14$, FULL was bit-exact over
6,000 instructions on Pong, Breakout, and Space
Invaders (supplement). LEAKY uses $\alpha=5.5$,
$T=0.145$ just outside the sufficient region,
with mild forward divergence.

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:ste}
STE~\eqref{eq:ste} makes full-system forward error
zero for all finite $\alpha,T>0$, irrespective of
the bounds above. Its backward derivative is that
of the chosen soft computation, not the hard
machine's derivative.
\end{corollary}

\section{Mechanism, Intervention, Explanation}

The known mechanism is the bit-exact hard transition
map $\Phi_{\mathrm{H}}$. Ground truth for explanation
is an \emph{exact intervention}, not a gradient.
For a ROM byte, RAM cell, register, or input $u$,
replace its value by $v$ and rerun the hard machine
with all other conditions fixed:
\begin{equation}
\Delta y(u;v)=
\Phi_{\mathrm{H}}(\ldots,\mathrm{do}(u:=v),\ldots)-y.
\label{eq:intervention}
\end{equation}
Here $\Phi_{\mathrm{H}}$ denotes execution to the
observation time and $y$ the original output.

We distinguish four output regimes.
\emph{Content} comprises register, colour, and
graphics-bit values, where selected-value gradients
are valid. \emph{Position} comprises sprite coordinates
set by strobe timing, where naive gradients through
indices are provably zero. \emph{Event} comprises
collision latches and lost lives, also with zero
naive gradients. \emph{Score} records game scores.

For a method $M$, faithfulness $F\in[0,1]$
measures correlation-based agreement between its
per-cause attribution $a_M(u)$ and the exact
intervention magnitude $|\Delta y(u;v)|$:
$F=1$ denotes exact agreement, $F=0$ chance.
Naive gradients, STE surrogates, samplers, and
fully relaxed gradients are \emph{methods under test},
never the reference. Exact forward equivalence
does not establish their faithfulness; comparison
with interventions does.


\section{Evaluation Methodology}

We evaluate 64 ALE games in the \textsc{xitari}
configuration. Canonical DQN used 49 games
\citep{mnih2015human}; later work used Atari-57
\citep{machado2018revisiting}. Each game follows the
standard ALE boot (60 NOOP frames, four resets),
then a fixed action stream. We compare all 128 RAM
bytes on each of 30 frames and the full
$210\times160$ frame buffer on each of 60 frames.
RAM- and pixel-exactness require zero differences
on every comparison. Claims concern these tested
trajectories, not all possible executions.

For conformance, we patched \textsc{xitari}'s
Superchip RAM seed from \texttt{time(NULL)} to zero,
matching both ports. The patch ships with the code.
One step denotes one CPU instruction, not an ALE
frame. CPU measurements use an Apple M1 Max core;
GPU measurements use a GTX 1080 Ti / Quadro RTX 5000
with peak batch $N\approx4096$. Batched
\textsc{jaxtari} uses the soft CPU core with no TIA,
a single ROM window, and only the RIOT timer.
Its hard path is eager and cannot be GPU-batched.
Full-system steps run eagerly on CPU in both ports;
Zygote reverse-mode throughput is measured in
\textsc{jutari}. The JAX GPU numbers remain
soft-CPU-core only.

\section{Results}

Both ports reproduce \textsc{xitari} on all 64 games:
64/64 RAM-exact over 30 frames and 64/64 pixel-exact
over 60 frames.

In \textsc{jutari}, full-system SOFT-STE equals HARD
on every instruction across 64 ROMs (3,000 each,
starting 20 frames into play), with zero uncorrected
soft-handler disagreements in CPU registers or RAM.
In \textsc{jaxtari}, the same holds on 9 ROMs
covering every exercised mapper family (1,500
instructions each, after standard boot and 20 frames).
The \textsc{jutari} checks exposed and fixed functional
\texttt{BRK} and RAM-executed-code bugs. In \textsc{jaxtari},
\texttt{jax.grad} through 100 full-system steps
reaches ROM bytes.

The full-system soft step reaches ${\sim}146{,}000$
instructions/s on CPU. Zygote reverse-mode gradients
are practical for 50--400-instruction windows, at a
few hundred instructions/s. The batched
\textsc{jaxtari} soft CPU core reaches ${\sim}3.1$M
instructions/s on GPU with ${\sim}5\%$ gradient
overhead; this is not full-machine differentiable
rollout throughput.

Figure~\ref{fig:xai} compares gradients with exact
interventions. Starting 35\,s after boot ($x=35$),
we hold RIGHT for 81 frames to reach the midpoint
$x=76$ of the measured range $[35,117]$, avoiding
wall clipping. The scene is pixel-exact against
the reference. The joystick drives position directly
through the sampler, bypassing ROM controller-reading
code. Naive differentiation gives zero; the bilinear
sampler localises screen gradients to cannon edges.

In panel (d), each series is normalised by its largest
magnitude because units differ. The sampler gives
RIGHT $+1.23$, LEFT $-1.23$, and UP/DOWN zero,
identically for SOFT-STE and relaxed settings
$(\alpha,T)=(6,0.14),(5.5,0.145)$ (maximum
difference zero). Naive gradients vanish throughout.
Hard-emulator interventions give RIGHT $+0.5$ and
LEFT $-0.5$\,px/frame, with UP/DOWN zero; only cannon
pixels change (rows 186--195; 56 pixels).
Thus the sign/zero pattern agrees away from the wall,
but the surrogate magnitude and joystick scale are not
game dynamics. At the wall, LEFT causes no pixel changes
despite the same nonzero sampler gradient. Exact
interventions, not gradients, provide the reference.

\section{Discussion}

The ports reproduce all tested reference trajectories.
SOFT-STE is forward-exact by construction
(Theorem~\ref{thm:exact}); the rounded relaxed variant
is also exact within the conditions of
Proposition~\ref{prop:finite}. Neither result
guarantees attribution faithfulness, as the
intervention tests demonstrate.

The authors designed the hard/soft split and
full-system STE step, the stored-switch
(max-pooling) view restricting surrogates to
discrete indices, the three conformance harnesses
using two independent ports as the oracle, and
the program-independent $\alpha/T$ bounds.
Agents wrote and debugged most code under this
oracle. Conformance exposed \textsc{xitari}'s
time-dependent Superchip RAM seed; the deterministic
seed-zero patch ships with the code.

\paragraph{Limitations and next steps.}
Conformance covers tested trajectories, not all
executions. Audio is absent. \textsc{jutari}
gradients are practical for short windows; GPU
batching applies only to the soft CPU core, not
full-system differentiation. The next step is
to intervene on a game variable, re-render
bit-exactly, and measure the resulting change in
a learned agent's action. Calibration on the
known machine must come first.

\section{Conclusion}

We provide a complex, fully known machine for
mechanism-grounded XAI. Two Atari VCS ports
reproduce the reference on 64 tested games.
A full-system soft step preserves exact forward
execution while supplying surrogate gradients.
Exact interventions expose where those gradients
agree with the known mechanism and where they fail.
This provides a reference for testing explanations
rather than relying on their plausibility.

\section*{Ethical Statement}
This work involves no human subjects, personal data, or sensitive information. The
commercial Atari ROMs are used only as fixed inputs; they are copyrighted and
\emph{not} redistributed---we release SHA-256 hashes for identification only. The
agentic method is dual-use; pairing autonomy with a strict, automatically checked
conformance oracle mitigates its central risk, silent divergence from the
reference, without eliminating it.
